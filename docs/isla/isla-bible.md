# Isla: protagonist bible (draft)

Who Isla is, so her voice has somewhere to come from. **None of this is shown to the player directly.** A therapist does not share her life with a child in session, and Isla keeps to that. It shows only through what she wears, what she carries, how she sits, what she will and will not do, and a few small disclosures that are safe to make. Items marked **(decision)** can change.

## 1. Who she is

**Dr. Isla Marlow**, 39. Children call her Isla. A licensed **child clinical psychologist** at Willow House in Philadelphia, where she has worked for five years. She is patient, dry, observant, and a little private. She has a habit of waiting a half-second longer than other people before she answers.

**Why she does it.** She believes children tell the truth with their hands before their mouths: which toy goes where, what they line up, what they will not step on. She likes the ordinary, specific moment more than the big one. Her rule is that she does not fix, she keeps company. What she wants, and does not quite say to herself, is to be the adult who does not need a child to be okay for her sake.

**What she is not.** She is not a rescuer, a wise guru, or a miracle worker. She is not always right, and she gets things wrong with children, and she knows it.

## 2. Where she comes from **(decision)**

- **Small-town Pennsylvania.** Grew up in a town outside Reading, the older of two. Her mother was a hospital nurse and her father an electrician. A quiet, steady household with not much said out loud.
- **Her brother, Danny**, is two years younger. He did not speak until he was four, and was later diagnosed autistic. He is now a grown man who runs a **bike repair shop** in another town. He speaks little by choice, is very funny when he does, and calls her every Sunday. He built her bicycle.
- **How she learned to listen.** As children, the only thing that reached Danny was a picture book he made her read **two hundred times**. She learned to read him through what he did: how he lined things up, where he sat, what he went back to. She learned to sit next to a person instead of facing them, and not to flinch at repetition. This is where her whole method comes from. She never tells anyone, because Danny's life is not a story she owns.

## 3. How she got into the field **(decision)**

1. **Children's librarian** at a public branch library, from 24 to 28. She ran a Saturday story hour and noticed that the children who would not speak would come back every week for the same book.
2. **Back to school**, 28 to 33, for a doctorate in child clinical psychology. She worked part time shelving in the university library to cover rent.
3. **Postdoc** at a children's hospital, 33 to 34.
4. **Willow House**, since she was 34.

She did not choose a path with money in it. She chose the one that fit.

## 4. Money and her life **(decision)**

- **Willow House pays a modest salary,** low for a psychologist and enough to live on. Not enough to be comfortable.
- **Student loans** are still being paid off.
- She rents a **one-bedroom** apartment on a bus line, two stops from work. She often rides the bike Danny built, in all weather, with a pannier for the books.
- **Her one extravagance is picture books.** Willow House's budget does not cover them, so she buys them herself and does not claim the money back. The shelf in the playroom is mostly hers.
- She supplements a little by running a monthly workshop for parents at the library she used to work in.

So: yes, it is enough, and she is careful. Nobody who sees her would think of her as struggling. Nobody would think of her as well off either.

## 5. Her personal life **(decision)**

- **Single,** and not lonely. She dates occasionally and does not talk about it. She has **close friends**: two women from her library years, with whom she has a Thursday dinner that has outlasted three apartments.
- **No children.** It is not a loss she dwells on, and it is not nothing. When parents ask whether she has children, she says, lightly, that she has "a lot of other people's, for an hour." She does not say more.
- **A very old cat named Footnote,** who she describes as "a retired colleague." He is the one safe personal fact she is willing to share with a child.

## 6. What she wears and carries

- **A cardigan** with ink-stained cuffs. She writes her notes **after** the session, never during, so the stains come from the end of the day.
- **Soft trousers and flat boots** she can sit cross-legged in on the floor.
- **Reading glasses** pushed up into her hair, usually forgotten there.
- **Nothing that dangles.** No necklaces, long earrings or loose sleeves, because children grab.
- **A canvas tote** from her library days, with a worn "Friends of the Library" patch. In it: five picture books she has read at least twice, a pencil stub, tissues, and a **small wind-up kitchen timer with a soft chime**. She winds it at the start so that ending a session is the timer's job and not hers.
- Her hair is tied back, sometimes with a pencil through it.
- **A square of oatmeal-colored cloth,** folded small in the tote. Before a story she draws it over the toy shelf.

## 7. How her background shows up in what she does

| What she does | Where it comes from |
|---|---|
| Sits beside a child, facing the toys, not facing them | learning to talk to Danny |
| Reads the same page again when asked, and never sounds bored | two hundred readings |
| Waits before answering | years of waiting for Danny to answer |
| Will not read a book she has not read twice | the librarian |
| Writes notes after, never during | she does not want to be a person who is writing |
| Covers the toy shelf with a cloth before a story: "Stories first. Toys are sleeping." | Danny could not look at a book with toys in sight, so she hung a towel over his shelf |
| Does not rush in with comfort | she learned that comfort too early ends the telling |
| Dry humor with adults, none at a child's expense | she has had to be good company to tired parents |

## 8. What she will and will not say to a child

**Will say (safe, brief):**
- She has an old cat named Footnote.
- Her favorite color (green).
- That she likes picture books a lot.
- That the timer ends the session, not her.

**Will not say:**
- Anything about her family, her brother, her past, her feelings, or her private life.
- "I understand." (She does not claim to.)
- "I had a similar experience."

If a child asks a personal question, she answers the safe ones in a sentence and turns back to the child. If it is a question she will not answer, she says so plainly: "That's one I keep to myself. I'll tell you about Footnote, though."

## 9. Her flaws

- **She over-prepares** and sometimes stays too long in her own head.
- **She keeps people at a distance** without meaning to. She is better at being a witness than at being known.
- **She takes the sentences home.** She has a notebook of things children have said, and she cannot stop reading it.
- After the hard sessions she sits on the back steps of the house, her bike leaning beside her, for five minutes before she rides home. The other clinicians have stopped asking.

## 10. Her voice, in one paragraph

Calm, dry, slow, and exact. She speaks to children in short, plain sentences, comments instead of asking, and uses their own words back. With adults she is warm and a little wry. She never tells anyone how to feel. She would rather say less and be right than say more and be nice.

## 11. Open questions

1. Is the brother's story right, or too convenient? It gives her method a source, and it opens the way to a later autistic patient written with care.
2. Is single-and-not-lonely right, or would a partner give her more warmth?
3. Is "five minutes on the back steps" after a hard session a good private detail, or too much?
4. Do you want her to have a surname the children know ("Dr. Marlow") or only "Isla"?

# Wren: story bible (draft, no scripted lines yet)

Everything the writing must stay true to. Lines come after this is agreed. Content stays everyday and non-graphic: nobody in this family is a villain, and nothing here is abuse. Items marked **(decision)** are choices I made that can change.

## 1. Who she is

**Wren**, six and a half (birthday in June). First grade. She has a loose front tooth and is proud of it. She is small for her age, careful with her hands, and counts things when she is worried.

**What a six-year-old can and cannot do** (the writing depends on this):
- Talks in short, concrete sentences. Strings events with "and then". Uses "because" loosely.
- Thinks in rules, fairness ("not fair", "same") and cause that is next to effect: if B came right after A, A caused B.
- Does not name feelings. She says her tummy goes funny, or she goes quiet, or she tidies.
- Treats toys as alive, and says true things through them.
- Cannot answer "how do you feel?" or "why?". She can answer "what happened?" and "show me".
- Gets sidetracked by something concrete (a crayon, a cartwheel) when a topic gets too big. That is not a lack of interest. It is how she steps back.

## 2. Where and how they live **(decision)**

**The place.** A rowhouse neighborhood in **Philadelphia**, Pennsylvania: narrow brick houses, front stoops, small back yards, a corner store and a bakery on the commercial street two blocks away. A working- and middle-income neighborhood where neighbors know each other. It is November, so it is cold and the leaves are down.

**Money.** **Lower-middle to middle class, and stretched.** Nobody is poor, and nobody is comfortable.
- **Mom (Nadia), 34**, is a **classroom aide** at a public elementary school across town. Steady hours, modest pay, and the family's health insurance through the school district.
- **Dad (Ben), 36**, is a **carpenter** for a small remodeling company: kitchens, porches, stairs. Busy in summer, thin in winter, and he has a truck.
- They bought the rowhouse seven years ago with help from Dad's parents for the down payment, and they still have the mortgage.
- Two households cost more than one. That is a quiet pressure on both of them now, and on Dad especially: he rents a small place and feels he has less to offer.

**Willow House** is a nonprofit children's counseling center. Fees are sliding-scale and partly covered by Mom's insurance. The parents did not have to choose between this and groceries, but it was a decision.

## 3. Her family and homes

- **Mom's house:** the family home. A narrow rowhouse with a **yellow** front door, a window seat in the kitchen at the back, and a small yard with a tomato plant that has finished for the year. Wren has lived there all her life.
- **Dad's place:** an apartment **above the bakery**, eight blocks away. It smells of bread from five in the morning. Dad built Wren a bunk bed and a shelf in the second bedroom. The street door is **green**. He chose it on purpose: close enough that Wren could keep the same school and walk between the two houses.
- **The schedule:** weekdays at Mom's. Tuesday nights at Dad's (he collects her from school). Every second weekend at Dad's. Handovers happen on the school's front steps or at a front door.

## 4. School **(decision)**

- Wren goes to the **local public elementary school**, three blocks from Mom's house, and **stays at the same school**. Her parents kept her there deliberately: it is the one thing that did not change.
- Everything else changed at once in late summer, because Dad moved out in August, right before the new school year. So first grade began with a **new teacher, a new classroom, and two homes**.
- Her teacher (Ms. Okafor) noticed she stopped sharing at morning meeting, and that she sorts the class library into piles at lunchtime. The school counselor suggested Willow House.
- After school, Wren goes to the school's after-care until Mom arrives at 5:30. On Tuesdays Dad comes at 3:30.

## 5. Extended family **(decision)**

- **Nana** (Mom's mother), 71, has heart trouble. She lives **40 minutes away** in a suburb. Mom drives out most Sundays, and her illness is part of why the last two years were hard. Wren loves her and is told Nana is "resting a lot".
- **Mom's grandmother** (who gave the blue cups as a wedding present) died when Wren was a baby. The cups were all Mom had of her.
- **Dad's parents** live in **Ohio**, a day's drive away. They helped with the down payment and call on Sundays. Dad has told them only a little.
- **Aunt Jo** (Dad's sister) lives ten minutes away with a large, clumsy dog named **Mustard**. She is Wren's easy place, and picks her up when a parent is stuck. She does not ask questions.
- Nobody in the extended family knows about the cup.

## 6. What really happened

**Before.** Mom and Dad were a happy couple who danced in the kitchen on Friday nights. For about two years that faded: Dad's winters of thin work, money worries, and Nana's heart. They never shouted in front of Wren. At night, through the floor vent, Wren heard the low, careful voices that mean "cross but quiet", and she learned to go still when she heard them.

**The cup (March).** Mom had four blue cups, a wedding present from her grandmother, and she loved them. One Saturday morning Mom was sad and trying not to show it. Wren wanted to help. She took one of the blue cups off the shelf, climbed on the kitchen stool, and made Mom a cup of tea, carrying it herself with both hands, very carefully. She dropped it at the kitchen door. It broke, and the handle came off whole.

Mom cried. It was not about the cup. It was about everything that week, and the cup was the last straw. Dad said, "It's only a cup, Nadia," meaning to comfort her. Mom heard that he did not care. They had the first real argument either of them had ever had in front of Wren, loud, in the kitchen. Wren sat on the bottom stair. She picked up the handle and put it in her pocket.

**After.** In **April** her parents sat her down and said, kindly and carefully, that they would live in two houses, and that it was not because of anything she had done. She heard "not because of anything you did" as the thing grown-ups say to be nice. Dad slept in the spare room all summer. In **August** he moved into the apartment above the bakery, and a week later she started first grade.

**The adults' truth.** The cup was a trigger and not a cause. The marriage had been ending for two years. Neither of them knows that Wren thinks the cup did it, because she has never said so. Nobody has ever asked her what *she* thinks happened.

## 7. What Wren believes

- **The cup caused it.** She broke it, then the shouting, then two houses.
- **It has to be fair.** She counts toys so each house has the same number. She sorts things into two bins, one for each house.
- **If she is very good and very careful, it might go back.** So she tidies, stays quiet, and does not make trouble.
- **Someone must not be sad because of her.** At Dad's she does not say Mom, and at Mom's she does not say Dad, because the person's face goes funny.
- **She is the one who must get the days right.** Once, in the first week of the new schedule, she said the wrong house. Dad waited on the school steps and Mom waited at home, and for an hour nobody came for her. Aunt Jo found her. She has checked the schedule every night since.

## 8. What each person knows about her

- **Mom:** she is tidy, quiet, no trouble. Worried about the stories.
- **Dad:** she is "a good little trooper".
- **Her teacher:** she stopped sharing at morning meeting, and sorts the class library into piles.
- **Isla:** the separation, the school report, and that her parents are both loving and anxious. **Isla does not know about the cup.** Nobody does.

## 9. Isla and the setting

Isla is a child psychologist at Willow House, a converted old house with a small waiting room. She reads picture books with children and watches what they do with them. This is **Wren's third session**:
1. Session one: Isla met the parents and Wren briefly.
2. Session two: Wren built a tower of blocks, in silence, then knocked it down and tidied every block.
3. Session three (the act): the first time Wren has come with a plan.

Mom waits in the waiting room. Dad brought her today and left her at the door.

## 10. The playroom

A pale green blind, so the light is soft. A low table. A beanbag. A shelf of picture books. A basket of crayons with one red crayon worn down to a stub, which Wren likes. **Two identical toy bins**, one at each end of the shelf. A rug with a road printed on it.

## 11. Wren's things

- **Rabbit** (named **Biscuit**), a small grey rabbit with one ear chewed. He goes everywhere with her and "never has to stay anywhere".
- **Rabbit's bag**, a small canvas satchel. In it: a toothbrush (she has two, and needs one in the bag in case of mistakes), a notebook, and the **blue cup handle**, wrapped in a sock.
- **The notebook.** She cannot write much yet. She draws a yellow square for a Mom day and a green square for a Dad day, and ticks each one. She checks it every night before bed.

## 12. The secrets, as discoveries

Each is something the player *finds*, through an object or a behavior, before she says it:

1. **The bag.** She has packed everything Rabbit needs so he is never left behind. *Rabbit is her.* She carries her own missing in the bag.
2. **The notebook.** The colored squares, and the story of the wrong-house day. She thinks keeping the days right is her job.
3. **The quiet.** She does not say each parent's name at the other's house. She tells Rabbit instead, in his chewed ear.
4. **The cup.** The handle in the sock. She tells the whole story in her own order, and then what she thinks it means.

She does not tell any of this in adult words. The adult meaning is never said aloud. The player puts it together.

## 13. How she talks

Rules for every line:
- Short sentences, four to eight words, joined by "and then".
- Concrete nouns and names (Rabbit, the green door, Tuesday). No adult summary words: no "responsible", "guilty", "sad about", "miss". She says "tummy goes funny", "face goes funny", "not fair".
- She does not answer feeling questions. She answers with action: she moves something, hides something, tidies.
- When a topic is too big she changes the subject to something specific and cheerful, such as a cartwheel or a crayon.
- She tells true things through Rabbit.
- American English: Mom and Dad, first grade, recess, sidewalk.

| What it means | What Wren would say or do |
|---|---|
| "I carry my missing in the bag." | Packs Rabbit's bag again. "Rabbit's got his toothbrush. In case." |
| "I think my job is to get the days right." | "It's Tuesday. Tuesday is the green door. I do it before bed." |
| "I hide missing each parent so they won't be hurt." | Whispers into Rabbit's ear, then: "That's a secret. He won't tell." |
| "I think I broke our family." | Takes out the handle. "It came off all in one bit." |

## 14. The book

*The Bird Who Carried Her Nest.* A small bird has a nest she loves. Two trees stand near each other, and she is afraid that whichever one she puts the nest in, the other will be sad. So she carries the nest around all day and never sets it down, and gets very tired. Other animals offer to hold it for her, and she says no. Then one evening it rains, and she is too tired to fly, and she sets it down on the ground between the trees, and nothing terrible happens.

Not a mirror of Wren's life. A story with the same shape.

## 15. The act and its ending

Arrival, play with the bins, the first look at Rabbit's bag, a picture book read aloud on the beanbag with Wren stopping and starting, the secrets as above, a closing, and then feedback: Wren leaves a drawing, and Mom adds a short note afterward. The authored review lines are picked by which secrets Isla held.

## 16. Open questions

1. Is a six-and-a-half-year-old the right age, or would seven or eight let her say more?
2. Is the loving-act version of the cup (she was making her mom tea) the right one, or too sad?
3. Is Philadelphia right, or would you rather a different city, a suburb, or an unnamed place?
4. Should Mom be present for any of the session, or only at the end?
5. What should the very last beat be: the drawing, the handle, or something Mom says?
